Photograph: AFP/Getty Images/Tang Chhin SothyCambodia This article is more than 10 years oldM&S and others supplied by factories that mistreat workers, rights group saysThis article is more than 10 years oldHuman Rights Watch report quotes complaints about forced overtime and underage labour at Cambodian factories also supplying Gap, H&M and AdidasEmma Graham-HarrisonThu 12 Mar 2015 16.30 GMTLast modified on Sat 2 Dec 2017 18.05 GMTShareCambodian factories supplying high street names including Marks and Spencer, Gap, H&M and Adidas are mistreating their workers, a rights group said, quoting employee complaints about abuse including forced overtime, firing of pregnant women and underage labour.Many of the 73 factories investigated by Human Rights Watch, for their report Work Faster or Get Out, were subcontractors in murky supply chains. That makes it much harder to hold the brands selling final products to account, the group said, calling for greater accountability.The group singled out M&S for particular criticism because of its secrecy about suppliers and because the firm “did not respond to the substantive concerns Human Rights Watch raised with them around subcontracting,” the group said. “By contrast H&M, Gap and Adidas responded to our concerns.”Some suppliers farm out work without permission, which makes it harder for big international companies to control worker conditions in those factories, HRW said, but the firms can still do more to tackle labour abuse.In interviews with 270 workers and dozens of other union and government officials and monitoring groups, HRW compiled a list of intimidation and ill treatment. Interviewees alleged that pregnant women in some factories were pushed out of work, and other factory workers were denied toilet or meal breaks, punished for taking sick leave or trying to organise unions, sexually harassed and kept on illegal short-term contracts.“They would call workers in and would get upset when they found out someone was pregnant and then they would fire those who were pregnant,” said Nheoum Soya, a worker in Phnom Penh. “I decided to abort my baby so that I could go back to work, so that I could be a good worker and maybe they would be happy with me.”Some factories were also using child labour, workers claimed, or exploiting teenagers who are old enough to do some work but are legally protected from overtime and the hardest tasks. One 15 year-old reported being ordered to hide from inspectors under piles of garments, and another told HRW she was told to stay home when “visitors” came.M&S told the Guardian they regularly audited suppliers in Cambodia, did not allow unauthorised subcontracting, had not been presented with any evidence to support the claims, and had responded to all of HRW’s questions in full.“All our suppliers must adhere to our strict ethical standards as a condition of working with us,” an M&S spokesman said. “All our supplier factories are audited regularly by third party, independent auditors and are visited by M&S compliance managers.”The company has not published details of its suppliers for commercial reasons, but has committed to providing a list of active suppliers next year, the spokesman added. HRW says it cannot name factories or individual workers because it would put them at risk of retaliation, making it harder to remedy individual wrongs, but said international firms should respond to the fundamental supply chain problems it highlighted.“Because of concerns for the job security of the workers involved, we do not provide the names of the factories implicated,” the report said. “We recognise that this makes it more difficult for the brands to respond to specific cases, but hope that this will encourage them to address the broader issues.”Publicly disclosed information showed H&M were sourcing products from 11 of the factories investigated, and Adidas from seven factories. Testimony, labels and documents gathered by HRW indicated several other firms that do not publish details of their supply chains were also getting products from the factories. M&S was supplied by 13 of the factories and Gap by five firms, HRW said.The group provided a detailed case study from several factories, anonymised to protect sources. At one, said to take regular orders from direct M&S suppliers at least until 2013 when workers were interviewed, the factory kept all employees on temporary contracts beyond legal limits.They refused to renew contracts for visibly pregnant women, punished any efforts to unionise and made heavy overtime demands, sometimes requiring workers to stay until 5.30am. “We cannot even speak of a union. Anyone who is slightly brave and challenges the factory is dismissed,” said Chhum Nary, a male worker.The factory also employed teenage workers by signing contracts with their parents. Although above the working age of 15, they were forced to do hours of illegal overtime. “Factory management allegedly maintained two sets of attendance records for children: one set that recorded the actual number of working hours to calculate wages, another to cover up overtime work for children,” the report said.Subcontractors in Cambodia’s $5.7bn garment export industry are regulated largely by the weak government watchdog, and escape stricter scrutiny from Better Factories Cambodia inspectors because that group is focused on monitoring companies that directly export their goods, HRW said.“Dealing with unauthorised subcontracting is complex,” the rights group said said. “But international apparel brands can do much more to help fix labour rights abuses in unauthorised production units brought to their attention.“H&M, Gap, and Adidas were the only brands Human Rights Watch contacted that acknowledged the concerns raised about subcontracting and reiterated their commitment to protecting and promoting worker rights throughout their supply chain.”HRW also called for better protection for whistleblowers, and efforts to remedy problems before dropping suppliers, as key to ensuring mistreated workers can report abuse without fear of losing their livelihood.Gap said: “We are investigating the alleged labour practices highlighted in this report. If true, they are unacceptable and violate our code of vendor conduct,” a spokeswoman said.Adidas said its efforts to manage its supply chain had been praised by HRW and it had relaunched its complaints mechanism last year in response to feedback.“The issues identified in Cambodia are not specific to our supply chain. Human Rights Watch is pleased with the Adidas group’s practices around transparency ... and complaint mechanisms, and is encouraging other brands to follow our approach,” the firm said in a statement.H&M said it was in close communication with HRW about labour conditions.
