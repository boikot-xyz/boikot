'Some people don't belong': The 'toxic culture' at Abercrombie after sex and racism claims

					Former CEO Mike Jeffries is facing allegations of sexual exploitation, but it isn't the first time the brand has been embroiled in controversy

Abercrombie & Fitch is at the centre of fresh controversy after a Panorama investigation shone a light on allegations of sexual exploitation and abuse involving the former CEO, Mike Jeffries.
But it is not the first time the all-American clothes retailer has hit the headlines for its reportedly toxic culture, racism and discrimination.
In the BBC’s The Abercrombie Guys: The Dark Side of Cool, broadcast on Monday night, eight men claimed they attended events hosted by Mr Jeffries and his British partner Matthew Smith, 60, at their New York residence and hotels between 2009 and 2015, where some were allegedly exploited or abused.
The pair have not responded to the allegations. Abercrombie & Fitch said it was “appalled and disgusted” by the alleged behaviour and has “zero tolerance for abuse, harassment or discrimination of any kind”.
Mr Jeffries, 79, took over as CEO of the company in the 1990s, rebranding it to focus on the teen customer and boasting of his “exclusionary” approach.
Mike Jeffries, former CEO of Abercrombie & Fitch, leaves the store on the Champs Elysees avenue in Paris in 2012, as some workers protest against working conditions (Photo: Bertrand Guay/AFP via Getty)
In a 2006 interview with the magazine Salon, he said: “In every school there are the cool and popular kids, and then the not-so-cool kids.
“Candidly, we go after the cool kids. We go after the attractive all-American kid with a great attitude and a lot of friends.
“A lot of people don’t belong, and they can’t belong.”
His idea of who belonged came to the fore in a 2022 Netflix documentary, White Hot: The Rise and Fall of Abercrombie & Fitch, which focused on allegations of discrimination at the firm.
The stores hired “models” rather than sales assistants, recruited for their looks, and store managers were asked to rank staff each week on how “cool” they were. Topless men posed with customers outside shops, with no plus-sizes sold until after Mr Jeffries departed as CEO in 2014.
Staff handbooks noted that dreadlocks – showcased on a black model – were banned, along with gold jewellery, while a “neatly combed, attractive, natural, classic hairstyle” – shown on a white model – was preferred.
Carla Barrientos told the documentary she was only given night shifts while working at an Abercrombie store in Bakersfield, California, involving stocktaking and cleaning windows. When she asked to swap to day shifts, she was dropped from the schedule completely.
A Netflix documentary in 2022 exposed a toxic culture at the firm (Photo: Netflix)
She said: “There wasn’t a resignation or anyone that called me. It was over.”
Jennifer Sheahan, in Irvine, California, told Netflix she was fired after a visit from bosses at A&F headquarters, who said there were too many Asian staff at the store. She said: “They noticed a bunch of Asian people in the store. They said ‘You need more staff who look like this’ and pointed to a poster and it was a Caucasian model.”
Both employees, along with seven others, sued A&F for race discrimination in 2003. The retailer settled for £30m and signed a court agreement to increase diversity.
Another former employee who joined the lawsuit, Anthony Ocampo, said he was told he couldn’t be rehired because “we already have two Filipinos working at this store”.
The brand was also notorious for logo T-shirts featuring offensive designs. One was emblazoned with racist caricatures of Asian men and read, “Wong Brothers laundry service – Two Wongs can make it white” while another featured a smiling Buddha and the words, “Abercrombie and Fitch Buddha Bash – Get your Buddha on the floor”. 
A series of court cases
Mr Jeffries’ tenure saw the brand become one of the biggest clothes retailers in the United States but it was also dogged by legal cases.
In June 2009, British law student Riam Dean took the company to an employment tribunal suing for disability discrimination.
She worked in the Savile Row store in London and claimed she was initially given special permission to wear clothing that covered her prosthetic limb but was then told her appearance breached the “Look Policy” and was relegated to the stock room. In August 2009, a tribunal ruled the 22-year-old was wrongfully dismissed and unlawfully harassed and was awarded £8,013 for loss of earnings and wrongful dismissal.

The same year in the US, 17-year-old Samantha Elauf said she applied for a sales assistant role at an Abercrombie Kids store but she said she was told by the manager her hijab breached the company’s “Look Policy”.
The United States Supreme Court heard the case and ruled 8–1 in June 2015 against the company.
Fran Horowitz was appointed as the company’s CEO in 2017.
Speaking after the Netflix documentary, she said: “Our ongoing evolution has been so rewarding, and we want to be clear that the recently released documentary is not reflective of who we are now.
“We own and validate that there were exclusionary and inappropriate actions under former leadership.
“Since I became CEO in 2017, we’ve overhauled Abercrombie and transformed with intention into a place of belonging.”
She said the organisation had “evolved”, making changes in management, prioritising representation, implementing new policies, and updating the ﬁt, size-range and style of its products.
“We’re focused on inclusivity,” she added.
